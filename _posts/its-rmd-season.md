For many investors entering their 70s, transitioning from a lifetime of contributing to tax-deferred retirement accounts quickly leads to having to make mandatory annual withdrawals. That's because the government requires investors to take required minimum distributions (RMDs) from such accounts after a certain age.

## **Old Rules vs. New Rules**




Among a number of changes, the recent SECURE Act 2.0 has changed the starting age for your RMD:

* **Born 1951 to 1959:** Commences at age **73**.
* **Born 1960 or later:** Commences at age **75**.
* **Born 1950 or earlier:** Commences at age **72** (or 70.5 if born before July 1949).




## **What To Do If You Have Multiple IRAs**







If you own multiple Traditional IRAs, the IRS allows you to calculate the Required Minimum Distribution for each account separately, but withdraw the total amount from any one or combination of those Traditional IRAs.




This rule provides flexibility in how you manage your underlying investments, but it comes with strict account-type limitations.




You can only aggregate and consolidate RMDs across the exact same account types. You **cannot** mix different categories of retirement accounts.




## **What To Do If You Have a 401k**







Unlike Traditional IRAs, you cannot aggregate 401(k) RMDs. If you have multiple 401(k) accounts from different employers, you must calculate and withdraw a separate RMD from each individual 401(k) account. Taking a larger distribution from one 401(k) does not satisfy the RMD requirement for another.




However, workplace plans offer a unique tax advantage known as the "still-working" exception, which allows some account holders to delay distributions entirely. 




If you are still actively employed past your RMD starting age, you can delay taking RMDs from your current employer's 401(k) until the year

 you finally retire.




## **Spousal and Inherited IRAs**







When you inherit an IRA, your distribution rules depend on whether you were married to the original account owner. Spousal beneficiaries have maximum flexibility, including the right to treat the inherited IRA as their own account, effectively delaying distributions until they reach their own RMD age or continue distributions based on their life expectancy.




Non-spouse beneficiaries have significantly stricter rules and are generally subject to a rigid 10-year depletion window.




## **The Bottom Line**







The SECURE Act 2.0 changes give your retirement nest egg more time to grow, but mismatched rules for IRAs, 401(k)s, and inherited accounts make proactive planning essential. Calculate your obligations early, consider account consolidation, and meet your year-end deadlines to optimize your tax strategy and completely avoid steep IRS penalties.
